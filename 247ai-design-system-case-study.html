Case Study · [24]7.ai · 2017–2018

Project Heisenberg — Standardisation of Enterprise Experience

When your platform is assembled through acquisitions, each team speaks a different visual language. Project Heisenberg was the design system that forced a shared grammar across 12 products — not by mandate, but by building it together.

Company
[24]7.ai
Timeline
April 2017 – September 2018
Role
Director of Design — Design System Lead
Scope
All product surfaces — voice, digital, chat, IVR

The Brief

The brief was precise: "Create a system to deliver experience for the [24]7 enterprise suite by standardising form, fit and finish." When I joined, [24]7.ai had been built through acquisitions. Teams that had never worked together were now expected to ship a unified platform. Components, colour, motion, and interaction patterns had evolved independently across six separate product lineages.

The result was a platform that looked and felt like six separate companies — because technically, it had been. A customer moving from chat to IVR to the agent console would encounter three completely different visual systems. Internally, teams had no visibility into how their design patterns would collide with adjacent products when customers crossed channels.

There was also a second structural problem: no data instrumentation in the console. Teams were designing blind — no usage signals, no performance feedback, no shared understanding of what was working across the platform.

The design system was the mechanism to solve both.

The Ecosystem It Had to Serve

Before defining any component, I mapped the full product landscape. [24]7.ai's platform spanned 12 products across two functional layers — On-Boarding (client setup, configuration, and the agent experience) and Optimisation (the intelligence layer that made the platform perform over time). A shared design language had to work across all 12, not just the most visible ones.

The product ecosystem Heisenberg had to serve — 12 products across two functional layers. Each circle is a product that had evolved its own visual language.

On-Boarding: Client Management, Tagging & Instrumentation, Visitor Experience Manager, Personalisation, Integrated Agent Console, Billing. Optimisation: Search Bidding, Campaign Management, Prediction as a Service, AIVA Content Manager, Workforce Optimisation, Reporting.

The design system had to hold across all of these — which meant the component and colour architecture couldn't be designed for the most obvious surface and retrofitted to the rest. It had to be designed for the hardest surface first.

How It Was Built

Where the design system work began — mapping the interconnections across the platform before defining how it should look.

I built Heisenberg ground-up and crowd-sourced — not handed down from a central authority. Every product team had ownership of the language they were using, which is why it actually got adopted rather than worked around. Teams contributed their existing patterns; we resolved conflicts in the open; what emerged reflected how the platform actually worked.

I ran all org-wide design sprints at [24]7.ai during this period. The sprints served double duty: discovery for specific product problems, and adoption mechanism for the design system. When teams worked together in a sprint, they encountered the design system in practice — as decisions to make together, not documentation to read. That's how adoption happened.

Timeline: April 2017 to September 2017 for the initial build with the core team. Platform-wide adoption continued through September 2018 as each product team integrated the system into their surfaces.

What Was Built

Eight semantic palettes — each with defined roles across product surfaces. Not brand colours, but a functional colour architecture for a multi-channel, multi-client platform.

Component namespaces

The library was organised into four namespaces, each designed for a distinct surface context:

UDE — Unified Design Environment
Core components for the agent console and operator surfaces — the highest-stakes interfaces where agents handle live customer interactions.
Chat
Components for the customer-facing chat widget — the interface 37 enterprise clients' end-customers interacted with directly.
Navigation
Global navigation patterns, wayfinding, and product-switching components — the connective tissue between 12 products.
Themeroller
Client-configurable theming engine — the system that let Nike Football, Cisco, Optus, and others customise their widget's visual identity within the design system's constraints.

Client configurability — Themeroller and VCC

[24]7.ai served 37 enterprise clients, each with their own brand identity. The design system had to enforce visual consistency at the platform level while giving clients enough configurability to feel like their own product.

The Themeroller namespace solved this by defining what was configurable (colours, logo, typography) and what wasn't (component structure, interaction patterns, accessibility). The Virtual Customer Console (VCC) gave client teams a WYSIWYG builder to configure their widget appearance within those constraints — so Nike Football's chat widget looked like Nike Football, but behaved like [24]7.ai.

The VCC card library — content cards (CARD, RESPONSE, CONVERSATION, PROTOCOL, WRAP, FORM) that clients used to configure their customer experience within the design system's constraints.

Design Principles

Three principles were named during the project — not decorative values statements, but working tools that teams used to resolve component disputes.

Focused Calm

The platform handled high-stakes customer interactions — frustrated customers, high contact volumes, agents under pressure. The interface had to reduce cognitive load, not add to it. Every component decision was tested against this: does this help the person do their job, or does it demand their attention?

Tone of Voice

Consistent language patterns across all system states, error messages, and empty states. With 37 enterprise clients and multiple products, language inconsistency was endemic. The Tone of Voice framework gave teams a shared vocabulary for how the system communicates — not just what it shows. A timeout error on the WFM console should sound like the same company as a connection failure in the agent chat widget.

Taxonomy in Play

Naming conventions for components, data entities, and user-facing labels applied consistently across all product surfaces. When the same data element was called four different things across four product teams, the design system created the forcing function to resolve that — not through decree, but through shared documentation that teams had built together.

The Governance Model

A design system without governance is a snapshot, not a system. The governance model defined how new components moved from a team's need to the shared library — and who had authority at each step.

1
Proposal
Any product team could propose a new component by documenting the need, the context, and the existing pattern (if any). Proposals were visible to all teams — preventing duplicate components from being built in parallel.
2
Cross-team review
Adjacent teams weighed in: does this conflict with an existing component? Does another surface need the same thing? Can this be generalised? The design sprint cadence was the natural forum for these conversations.
3
Resolution in the open
Conflicts were resolved with the teams that had skin in the game — not in a central design system team's backlog. This is what "crowd-sourced" actually meant: the decision authority sat with the people who would live with the result.
4
Adoption through sprints
New components were introduced in design sprints — used in practice before they were documented as standards. This meant teams encountered the design system as a tool that helped them, not a constraint handed down after the fact.

The Platform in Practice

The design system applied across the platform — AIVA console, WFM, client management, and reporting sharing the same visual grammar for the first time.
