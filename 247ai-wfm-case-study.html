The ecosystem nobody had mapped

I joined [24]7.ai as design leader for the data platform. My job was described as making reporting dashboards better. Within weeks, a different picture emerged: the people who most needed information had no access to it at the moment they needed it.

The contact centre ecosystem is not simple. Research revealed 14 distinct roles — each with different responsibilities, different data needs, different tolerances for system complexity, and different relationships with the people around them. WFM, QA, team leads, trainers, agents, planners — none of them were using the same tool in compatible ways. They were operating in parallel, loosely connected, with gaps everywhere between roles.

Role interconnection diagram — chord chart showing the volume of communication flows between 14 contact centre roles. Agent and Team Lead carry the highest interconnection load. WFM sits at the intersection of scheduling, rostering, and real-time management but is rarely designed for that centrality.

Size of operation — ratio view. The Agent bubble dominates by orders of magnitude. Team Lead 1:60; Manager 1:60; WFM 1:90; QA 1:50; Trainer 1:60; HoP 1:990. Every tool decision that favours the procurer over the agent is a decision that degrades experience for the single largest group of users in the system.

The research method was follow-the-user for a full day, across roles: diary studies, observation of system parts that had not been updated in years, and documentation of the gaps between what tools claimed to do and what people actually needed them for. The interconnection diagram above came from that research — it's not a framework, it's a map of what we actually observed.

What we found: five specific problems

Before touching a screen, the research surfaced five distinct failure modes — each quantified, each traceable to a design gap, each fixable through the right product decision.

20%
Break management → abandoned chats
Humans connect with other humans and tend to take breaks together. Without a system that managed break patterns against live chat volume, real-time managers were losing 20% of total chats to uncoordinated breaks. The system needed to care for people while optimising for the operation simultaneously.
40 min/hr
Attendance chaos for real-time managers
RTMs spent ~20 min/hour finding the next shift's agents and confirming attendance, then another 20 min finding replacements for absent or delayed agents. Three manual options: hold breaks of existing agents, ask supervisors to log in, convince agents to stay late — none of them fast, none of them data-informed.
No mapping
All chats treated equal, all agents treated equal
The system had no skill mapping. Every incoming chat was routed the same way regardless of intent complexity. Every agent was measured the same way regardless of expertise. Performance management defaulted to NPS and resolution rate — the only metrics available when skills aren't tracked.
Overstaffing
Disconnected systems → blunt planning
Without real-time data on agent availability and arrival patterns, leads defaulted to overstaffing as the only reliable hedge. Overstaffing lowered abandonment rates but increased cost and drove up low-value non-crucial intent handling — volume without business benefit.

"All chats are considered equal / All agents' expertise are considered equal." The pre-WFM state — flattened treatment of both demand and supply. No intent classification driving routing, no skill mapping driving assignment. Volume as the only measure of throughput.

Attendance and availability at shift start — the RTM's manual decision tree when an agent is absent or delayed. Three options, all manual, all slow, all compressing the 20-minute window before the shift gap becomes a service level breach. The framing: demand and supply

Before designing anything, I needed a conceptual model that the whole team could work from. The framework that emerged from the research: workforce optimisation is a demand-supply matching problem.

Demand is the intent of each contact — what the customer actually needs, arriving through chat, phone, voice, or IoT. Supply is the resolution capacity — human agents with specific skills, virtual agents with specific capabilities, routed on the basis of intent match rather than simple queue position.

The product needed to operate across three time horizons simultaneously: long-term availability (weeks out), rostering and scheduling (this week), and real-time management (right now). Each horizon had different users, different decisions, and different tolerance for error. Getting the sequence of product development right — which to build first — was the first design decision.

I proposed a maturity model: start with real-time schedule adherence (highest immediate pain), then build toward forecasting. What actually shipped first was forecasting — driven by a business sequencing decision. The product team built the planning layer first, then closed back toward the floor-level tools. This shaped everything about how adoption worked.

The full WFM ground process — mapped before any screen design. Three mental models — three separate products

The research and the ground process map together revealed the central insight: agents, supervisors, and workforce planners weren't using the same tool in different ways. They had fundamentally incompatible mental models of what the tool was for. No vendor had designed for all three simultaneously — they built for the manager or the planner and bolted agent access on as an afterthought.

Self-serve as cost reduction — the business case for good UX

The WFM tool historically required manager involvement for things agents could handle themselves — swap requests, time-off queries, schedule checks. Making the tool genuinely self-serve was both the right UX decision and a measurable cost-reduction mechanism. Every approval that moved from manager to agent directly reduced operational overhead. This framing got internal buy-in in a way that UX improvement arguments alone never did.

The gaps in the middle section show the coverage drop that results from synchronised (non-staggered) breaks.

How it got built

The Workforce Optimisation initiative started as an internal tool — an extension of the Insights platform to support near-real-time forecasting. The MVP launched in April 2017 with 3 designers and 3 product managers rotating through the initiative. The full product reached market by 2020.

One structural challenge hit immediately: Workforce Optimisation couldn't share a cloud with Insights. Near-real-time refresh requirements meant it had to sit closer to agent systems, process data separately — and therefore use a separate authentication system. Agents already managing too many systems would now need two logins. It took 1.5 years and a platform-level initiative (OKTA via the "Central" team) to solve this.

Agent performance and skill betterment

The platform's intelligence went beyond scheduling. As WFM matured, it connected the workforce planning layer to individual agent performance — making skill-based routing possible and giving team leads the data to have meaningful coaching conversations.

The research finding was stark: skills weren't mapped anywhere in the system. Every agent was measured the same way, every chat was treated the same way. Performance management defaulted to whatever proxy data existed — NPS and resolution rate — even when those metrics didn't distinguish between a complex technical query resolved in one touch and a simple password reset handled in five minutes.

Agent performance view — Hilton Sales, 19 Sept 2018. Individual agent metrics: Score (44–92) · AHT · Conversion % · Sales · Total Chats · Revenue. Team ABCO, 14 agents. This view gave team leads the data to coach on specific gaps — not on overall NPS, but on individual skill performance against the metrics that actually mattered for the client's business.

Agent self-serve portal — schedule grid, swap request management, real-time adherence. Built for a floor context: maximum three primary actions, operable without training. Every approval that moved from manager to agent was a measurable reduction in supervisory overhead.
